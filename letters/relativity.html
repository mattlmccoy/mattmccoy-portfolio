Full-Lifecycle Program Experience

As Technical Program Manager for the Fuse 1+ 30W SLS printer at Formlabs, I owned the program from first hardware builds through production release. That meant holding the system-level view across subsystems that each had their own technical leads: optics, thermal management, powder handling, firmware, software, and factory test. My job was to make sure their interfaces were correct, their requirements were verified before they became assumptions baked into hardware, and that integration failures surfaced as early as possible rather than at first-article testing. We managed more than 130 engineering change orders through the NPI cycle and shipped in nine months from prototype to production. The resulting platform went on to capture approximately 55% of the global SLS market by unit volume.

The factory test infrastructure I oversaw was responsible for more than 150 per-unit acceptance tests. Building that into a reliable production gating process required the same rigor systems engineering asks for in any program: clear requirements, defined verification methods, and alignment from the engineering team that the test actually covers the failure modes that matter. When it didn't, I ran the root cause investigation and drove the corrective action. Over my time at Formlabs I authored more than 30 technical reports documenting those investigations, driving engineering decisions across optics, thermal, and process subsystems.

Systems Thinking Across Disciplines

SLS is a genuinely multi-physics system: a laser delivery system with variable-focus optics, a thermal management architecture maintaining powder bed temperature within tight tolerances, a powder handling system coupling mechanical and thermal behavior, and firmware and software tying it together. Understanding how changes in one subsystem propagate through the others is the core skill of systems engineering, and it's the skill I developed most at Formlabs. When an optical specification changed, I had to understand the thermal implications. When a firmware change affected scan timing, I had to understand the impact on part quality. Building and maintaining that cross-subsystem view, and making sure the team's individual decisions stayed coherent at the system level, was the hardest part of the job.

My doctoral research at Georgia Tech has pushed this further. The RFAM platform I built from scratch integrates sub-100 µm motion control, high-viscosity inkjet deposition, a 600W 13.56 MHz RF energy delivery system, and a scanner-based diagnostic platform into a single closed-loop research environment. The system couples electromagnetic field behavior, heat transfer, and material phase change in ways that interact across every subsystem. Designing experiments that isolate variables in a system like that, and building the diagnostic infrastructure to understand what's actually happening, is close to what systems engineering does on a launch vehicle: you're trying to understand a complex system well enough to make confident predictions about behavior you haven't observed yet.

Building and Leading Technical Teams

At Formlabs I transitioned from Technical Program Manager to Technical Lead for the next-generation SLS platform, with direct responsibility for the technical direction of the program's highest-risk subsystem. When the program stalled in architectural uncertainty, I scoped and executed an independent technical investigation that produced the first concrete engineering foundation for the program and drove the decision to greenlight development. That role required a combination I try to build in the engineers I mentor: willingness to own hard questions without a clear answer, discipline to run the right experiments rather than the comfortable ones, and clarity in communicating results to people who need to make decisions.

Mentoring junior engineers toward that kind of rigor is something I take seriously. In my Ph.D. lab I've worked with several undergraduate and master's students, helping them scope research questions appropriately, design experiments that give interpretable results, and communicate technical work clearly. The transition from "smart person working independently" to "engineer who makes the team around them better" is one I find the most important to develop early, and it's the main thing I focus on in mentorship.

Process Rigor in Fast-Moving Programs

The tension Relativity's job description names explicitly, rapid development pace against engineering rigor, is one I've navigated throughout my career. At Formlabs, the pressure to ship fast was constant and real; the answer was never to skip verification but to be much more deliberate about which verification activities mattered most and when they needed to happen. I ran Gage R&R studies to understand measurement system capability before accepting data as meaningful. I designed calibration protocols that caught process drift before it produced field failures. I structured ECO reviews to catch interface problems at the change level rather than the integration level. The goal was always to build quality into the process rather than inspect for it at the end, because at Formlabs' production volumes, the end was too late.

That same philosophy applies to a launch vehicle program operating under ambitious timelines. The value of a systems engineering function isn't slowing things down; it's ensuring that the team's pace doesn't accumulate technical debt that surfaces at a stage gate or on the pad. I'd bring that perspective to the Terran R program alongside the technical depth to have credible conversations with the subsystem teams about where the real risks are.

Relativity Space is building something genuinely hard, and the systems engineering challenge of holding Terran R together as a coherent system through rapid development is the kind of problem I'm ready to work on. I'm prepared to relocate to Long Beach and I'd love to talk through the role in more detail.
