Field-Deployed Metrology and Diagnostic Systems

A central challenge for professional SLS is environmental variability. Factory calibration alone cannot account for the geographic and seasonal thermal shifts that alter powder behavior and part accuracy in real-world deployments. I addressed this by developing two novel diagnostic systems designed for direct customer use.

The first was a dimensional accuracy tool that used inexpensive consumer-grade calipers and purpose-designed calibration artifacts to reduce dimensional error by a factor of three, improving accuracy from ±300 µm to ±100 µm, without requiring on-site technician visits that typically cost thousands of dollars each. This system directly enabled LEGO's global factory deployment of Fuse printers. The second was a thermal diagnostic tool using engineered geometric features embedded in printed artifacts to reverse-engineer print parameters and detect thermal drift inside the machine. Both tools remain in active use more than three years after deployment and were recently featured publicly by Adam Savage. This experience, building metrology systems that quantify dimensional accuracy, detect process drift, and track quality metrics at fleet scale, maps directly to the post-build analysis and tool qualification work at the core of this role.

Inventing a New Manufacturing Process from Scratch

Your posting emphasizes passion for developing processes from scratch. My doctoral research at Georgia Tech, funded by the NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, is exactly that. I pitched the concept for Radio Frequency Additive Manufacturing (RFAM), a process that replaces raster laser scanning with selective dopant deposition followed by volumetric RF heating, secured approximately $150,000 in research funding, and built the first RFAM platform from the ground up within a single year.

The system I designed and built integrates sub-100 µm motion control, high-viscosity inkjet deposition capable of suspending 50 wt% dopant loading, a 600W 13.56 MHz RF energy delivery system with Faraday isolation, and a scanner-based diagnostic platform for quantifying both dimensional accuracy and dopant distribution. Using Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Models, the system classifies ink concentration with 98.9% accuracy while simultaneously measuring spatial placement errors.

This work lives in the kind of complex multi-physics space Freeform operates in: electromagnetic field interactions with spatially heterogeneous material systems, energy coupling and heat transfer, and the challenge of understanding how process parameters map to final part properties. I've built the experimental infrastructure, the data pipelines, and the statistical frameworks to explore that space rigorously.
