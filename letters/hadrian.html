Dear Hadrian Hiring Team,

I'm writing to express my interest in the Technical Program Manager, Factory as a Service position. I've spent my career working as both a hands-on manufacturing engineer and a cross-functional program manager: leading multi-disciplinary teams through complex hardware programs at Formlabs while building a real foundation in process physics and advanced manufacturing. Rebuilding American manufacturing capacity for aerospace and defense through autonomous precision machining is work I care about, and the FAAS model is a high-stakes operational program challenge I want to be running.

Program Management Across Complex, Cross-Functional Teams

At Formlabs I assumed Technical Program Management ownership of the Fuse 1+ 30W SLS platform three months into my role. I coordinated more than 30 engineers across mechanical, electrical, firmware, software, and manufacturing process disciplines, managing over 130 engineering change orders through the full NPI cycle. Simultaneously, I oversaw the factory test software team responsible for more than 150 acceptance tests per production unit, working closely with Operational Program Management to align test coverage with production throughput targets and ship milestones. We delivered Formlabs' fastest-ever printer launch: prototype to production in nine months, resulting in a platform that captured approximately 55% of the global SLS market by unit volume.

Managing that program taught me the discipline of program execution under pressure: maintaining a living view of scope, timeline, and risk across teams with very different working cadences; distinguishing between risks that needed immediate escalation and those that needed structured mitigation plans; and communicating status accurately to leadership without false precision. The hardest moments weren't the technical problems. They were the moments when the technical picture was uncertain and I had to give decision-makers something they could act on anyway.

Technical Depth as a Program Management Advantage

The best program managers I've worked with could read an engineering team accurately: knowing when "we're on track" means "we're on track" and when it means "we haven't looked at that yet." My engineering background lets me have those conversations at the level of the actual technical problem. At Formlabs, when the next-generation SLS program stalled in architectural uncertainty, I didn't wait for the team to surface a path forward: I independently scoped and executed a technical investigation of the program's highest-risk subsystem and produced the first concrete engineering foundation for the program direction. That investigation unblocked development and gave leadership the confidence to greenlight the program.

For FAAS programs at Hadrian (coordinating factory buildouts, managing CapEx delivery timelines, maintaining customer-facing program communication), this matters. I can read a machining floor's status the way I can read an R&D lab's: where the real constraints are, which delays are recoverable, and how to communicate with customers in a way that's honest without being alarming.

Deploying Systems at Scale, Across Sites

A significant portion of my work at Formlabs was operationalizing manufacturing systems across a distributed global installed base. I developed two field-deployed diagnostic and calibration tools designed for direct customer use across thousands of systems in geographically dispersed environments. The first reduced dimensional error by a factor of three (from ±300 µm to ±100 µm) and enabled LEGO's global factory deployment of SLS printers. The second was a thermal diagnostic tool for detecting process drift across field-deployed machines without on-site technician visits. Both required careful thinking about deployment logistics, user workflow, and how to make quality monitoring reliable when you can't control the environment.

The FAAS model raises the same kinds of challenges: how do you maintain program visibility and quality across multiple factory locations simultaneously? How do you structure customer communication across programs at different stages of buildout? I've developed the project management and operational habits (structured reporting, clear scope documentation, milestone-driven execution) to keep multi-site programs coherent and stakeholders aligned.

Operating in Fast-Moving, Ambiguous Environments

Formlabs during my tenure was a fast-growing startup operating at the edge of what its manufacturing and engineering organization could support. Program scope changed, priorities shifted, and the distance between "prototype" and "production" was never as clean as the schedule suggested. My response to that environment was to build structure where it didn't exist: establishing clear ownership for subsystem decisions, creating shared tracking artifacts that gave cross-functional teams visibility into each other's status, and holding regular alignment checkpoints that kept the program honest without becoming bureaucratic overhead.

My doctoral research at Georgia Tech reinforced those habits in a different context. I pitched the concept for Radio Frequency Additive Manufacturing, secured approximately $150,000 in research funding including the NSF GRFP, and built the first RFAM platform from scratch within a year, managing scope, timeline, budget, and technical risk without a formal program management structure. Running programs in ambiguous environments with no support structure is something I've had to figure out, and it maps well to early-stage FAAS deployments where the playbook is still being written.

Rebuilding American manufacturing capacity for aerospace and defense is one of the more important problems in the sector right now, and the FAAS model is a smart way to scale it. The combination of customer-facing program management and operational factory delivery is the kind of work I'm most interested in doing next. I'm prepared to travel extensively and to hit the ground running on programs already in motion.

I'd be glad to talk through how my background maps to what you're building.
